# Dynamic Multi-Hierarchical Organizations: Literature Review

**Shubham Gumma and Kushaal Sharma** · September 2025

## Continuous-Time Dynamic Incentive Models

A principal–agent model studies how an employer (principal) can motivate a worker (agent) to act in the principal's interest when the agent's effort or actions are not directly observable. This hidden action problem is known as moral hazard, where one party has an inclination to take risks and the other party agrees to bear any costs that arise from these actions. In static (one-period) settings, classical contract theory shows that the principal can give the agent a performance-based payment to encourage effort, balancing risk and incentivization to optimally control individuals in the hierarchy. However, if we simply repeat this interaction over many periods, the problem becomes very complex with a curse of dimensionality emerging as infinitely many combinations of interactions/histories between the principal and agent develop over time.

In the 1980s, economists turned to continuous-time models to simplify dynamic incentive problems where hierarchical relationships were interpreted over an extended period of time. Holmström and Milgrom (1987) were pioneers in this approach, modeling the output of an agent's work as a continuous-time process with the inclusion of random noise (using Brownian motion), with drift arising from the agent's varying efforts in completing tasks. The explanation for drift is as follows: the drift can be related to the agent's output as a noisy signal that the principal observes continuously; as such, the harder the agent works, the higher the drift and expected increase of the output. Holmström and Milgrom showed that in this setting a linear contract — where a base salary is paid on top of a proportional bonus that is determined by observed output — can optimally balance incentives and risk-sharing under certain conditions. Under this model, efforts only affect the drift and the principal and agent are classified as risk-neutral and constant risk averse respectively. Their continuous-time framework was a breakthrough as it kept the computation for factors such as pay-rates in closed form solutions even as the horizon became indefinite. Subsequent work, such as Schättler and Sung (1993) and Müller (1998), refined the model and solution under various utility assumptions (such as CARA or CRRA), but Holmström and Milgrom's linear contracting insight remains foundational to the field.

A major advancement in continuous-time incentive theory came two decades later when economist Sannikov (2008) developed a new method to solve dynamic principal–agent problems by reformulating them as a single optimal control problem, bypassing the need to track full past histories. He introduced the idea of using the agent's promised future utility as a state variable in the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation, meaning that continuation utility could be adjusted by anticipated utility. Through this framework, the principal would only need to keep track of the agent's promised continuation payoff and adjust it based on performance. Sannikov also used the Martingale Representation Theorem to ensure that if the agent ever deviated, it would be detected through the output process. Specifically, changes in effort would alter the drift of the output processes and would subsequently affect outcomes. This approach drastically simplified the analysis of infinitely long or continuous relationships by embedding incentive compatibility conditions into the evolution of the promised payoff. Moreover, it overcame the complexity of multi-period models and allowed explicit solutions for optimal contracts in continuous time which were previously incomputable in closed form.

Early continuous-time models often assumed specific preferences (such as exponential utility) and confined the agent's action to affecting only the mean output. In the years following Sannikov, researchers expanded the theory to apply more general settings. Subsequently, this allowed for broader versions of Sannikov's model such that agent decisions could not only be traced to output but also risk. Williams (2015), for example, provided a "solvable" continuous-time model that accommodated more general utility functions and hidden states, something that was not possible under previously held rigid assumptions. His work and related papers demonstrated how, under certain technical conditions (agent utility and principal objective held as concave), the principal's problem could still be solved by exploiting concavity and dynamic programming, even without the restrictive assumptions of exponential utility and its standalone effect on drift established by Holmström–Milgrom. Around the same time, Cvitanić, Possamaï, and Touzi (2018) made significant contributions to generalizing continuous-time contract theory that involved extending an agent's control to the mean and riskiness of outcomes. Through this approach they were able to develop a novel framework for continuous-time contract design that simplifies the analysis by focusing on revealing contracts, making the agent's optimal response tractable without sacrificing generality. Rather than considering every possible contract, the researchers showed that simply considering a subset of representative contracts was viable from a computation perspective, without loss of generality. By doing so, the principal's optimization reduces to a standard control problem and allows cases where the agent controls not only the drift but also the volatility of output. In these extended settings built on Sannikov's foundational model, the optimal contracts necessarily include new features (such as rewards or penalties) that directly address an agent's ability to shape multiple factors of performance and establish a foundation for a unified continuous-time dynamic contract theory.

## Hierarchies and Delegation in Contract Theory

In many firms and institutions, there is a multi-level hierarchy with individual connections between principal and agent converging into a tree with various connected principal–agent subsets. This is most clearly seen in organizational structures where CEOs are at the top, a manager in the middle, and workers at the bottom. The top leader, often referred to as the principal, often cannot directly manage everyone, so they delegate authority by hiring middle managers, who in turn supervise lower-level employees and report up the command chain. As more layers develop in hierarchical structures where numerous webs of interactions occur between managers (who relay information from agents) and the principal, it raises speculation about the effect of such management on incentivization and subsequent performance by agents, given no direct supervision by the principal. Classic results in contract theory, like the Revelation Principle, suggest that the absence of frictions like communication costs or collusion allow a principal to interact with its agents without any loss. As such, hierarchy should not improve outcomes in a world of perfect contracting, but is vastly consequential to reducing real-world frictions (communication delays, limited span of control, local information, etc.).

Building on this insight, an influential early study by Melumad, Mookherjee, and Reichelstein (1995) examined a simple three-tier hierarchy: a principal, a middle manager, and a worker. They surprisingly determined that under certain ideal conditions, a hierarchy need not impose any efficiency loss compared to direct contracting. Specifically, if the intermediary (middle manager) has no binding wealth or liability constraints and the contract is verified in a top-down manner, then a three-layer arrangement can achieve the same performance as if the principal had directly contracted with the worker. This result suggested that hierarchies indeed work well — but it relies on strong assumptions of the manager and contract. Mookherjee and Reichelstein (2001) extended the analysis to larger hierarchies with multiple agents at each layer, showing that similar principles apply as long as intermediate managers can themselves be given high-powered incentives and there are no collusion opportunities. In turn, both these studies hypothetically confirmed that indirect contracting through managers was equally as effective as direct contracting.

On the flip end, researchers also identified conditions when delegation was more lucrative than centralization. Choe and Park (2004), for example, studied a model of a company with one principal, one manager, and one worker. In their setup, the manager had the ability to acquire valuable private information that the principal otherwise wouldn't get. They compared two scenarios: one where the principal contracts directly with both manager and worker (known as a two-tier centralized structure), and another where the principal contracts only with the manager, who in turn contracts with the worker (a three-tier delegated hierarchy). Choe and Park found that delegation can be beneficial to the principal in an environment where the manager's private information actually improves decision-making and incentivization for the worker. Therefore, making the manager an intermediary can motivate the manager to gather and use local information to better supervise the worker, something a distant principal might not achieve. However, they also noted that delegation should stop at the manager and that giving the worker the same autonomy did not help. The manager's dual role in both taking decisions and contracting with the worker was key to improving outcomes, highlighting that hierarchies add value when each layer performs functions (monitoring of agents or gathering local information) that the layer above cannot easily do alone.

In static models of hierarchies, researchers also examined issues like collusion and auditing, which looked into the potential negatives of hierarchical models. For instance, Tirole (1986) and Laffont and Martimort (1998) looked at how an agent and a supervisor might collude to deceive the principal, and what kind of auditing or incentive scheme can prevent that. Those works establish that having a supervisor (middle agent) can backfire if the supervisor can collude with the worker, unless the principal allocates some incentives for the supervisor to report truthfully. These collusion models, while insightful, usually consider a one-period interaction. As such, collusion and auditing in contexts of hierarchical models are yet to be studied on a long-term scale. Similarly, Kofman and Lawarrée (1993) emphasized the costliness of preventing collusion, as principals may need to fund audits or weaken incentives to keep supervisors honest. This highlights a disadvantage of hierarchies in static settings in that while they reduce the burden on the principal, they also decrease efficiency and increase cost.

## Dynamic Incentives in Hierarchies

Dynamic contract theory and hierarchy theory, for the most part, developed independent of each other. In the real world however, organizations often face multi-layer incentive problems that unfold over time. As a result, recent contributions have aimed to combine the two approaches and enhance their applicability.

Shin and Strausz (2010) first focused on the efficiency of a horizontal versus a vertical hierarchical system in motivating workers over time. They developed a dynamic model of organizational design under limited commitment, where the principal cannot commit to long-term wage contracts, meaning that they can renegotiate contracts over time. In the case of a horizontal hierarchy, a principal would directly manage multiple workers without an immediate boss, while a vertical hierarchy would involve a principal managing a senior agent, who then in turn would manage the others. The study found that the optimal structure varied based on the environment, with a fast-growing firm and high certainty in workers' abilities favoring a horizontal structure, and a slower-growing business and uncertain employee efficiency being better suited to a vertical structure. This was due to the increased ability of a vertical hierarchy to handle dynamic incentive issues under limited commitment due to the presence of a "middleman" boss who could help mitigate two dynamic problems. First, it would prevent junior workers from getting away with poor performance due to an immediate supervisor to monitor them, and second, it would prevent the principal from over-promising to high performers and holding them up later. While somewhat conceptual, the study emphasizes the importance of an intermediate agent in a dynamic setting, and shows that no one size fits all.

A study by Miller and Yang (2015) centered around reducing the mathematical complexity that arises when moving to fully dynamic models with multiple layers. They considered a hierarchy with N+1 players — a principal, and N agents in a chain, with each agent directly managing the one below. The study, based on an earlier method by Evans, Miller, and Yang (2015) identified certain conditions where the problem could be simplified, allowing one to collapse the huge state space of a multi-tier system to one "effective" state variable and one control, regardless of the size of the hierarchy. Essentially, this showed that if certain informational conditions hold, adding more layers would not make the contracting problem harder (at least from the principal's perspective). While a significant finding, however, the framework was not fully general. It relied on the assumption that agents only control the drift of some common output, and not more complex actions like risk, which as later research shows, is a crucial aspect in continuous-time models. Nonetheless, it was one of the first tractable solution methods for large-scale dynamic hierarchies, and demonstrated that dynamic programming techniques could, in principle, handle multiple layers. Miller and Yang's focus on the simplification of multi-layer hierarchies to evaluate them in a more condensed form was dependent on several assumptions and specific cases however. More recent work takes a different route by using learning methods to deal with complexity in multi-level systems, rather than simplifying the system itself. For example, Scheid et al. (2025) researched a multi-layer principal–agent setup where each principal gives simple one-step contracts to the agents below them in a tree structure. They found that even when agents act only for themselves, these local contracts can lead the whole system toward the best outcome, something that occurred over time. Using a no-regret learning algorithm, it was revealed that over time, agents adjust their choices to align with overall organizational efficiency. As such, this method was able to connect incentivization with online learning in bandit problems (situations where numerous future decisions have to be made with no insight into better outcomes) to serve as a roadmap to solve complex analytical models.

Yi Chen (2022) studied a model of dynamic delegation in a principal-agent relationship with a continuously evolving hidden state. The model has only one agent, but is conceptually similar to a hierarchical setup, with the principal delegating decision-making and choosing actions based on the agent's reported information. It captures the idea that the agent has private information that evolves over time, with the principal committing to an action plan depending on messages from the agent. He was able to solve the optimal contract in closed form. One insight from this work is that the optimal dynamic policy can sometimes be counterintuitive, with the principal taking actions that may be opposite to what immediate incentives suggest in order to induce better information from the agent. The study did not have an explicit three-tier hierarchy, but is useful in showing how dynamic incentive problems with informational asymmetry can be solved when a principal delegates some control to an agent over time. It reinforces the theme that dynamic contracts can be quite subtle, and can require complex strategies even in principal-one agent cases, with a hierarchy only compounding these challenges as an intermediate agent can strategically manage information flow from both sides. Similarly, Shu and Tian (2019) combined reinforcement learning with contract theory in a multi-agent environment. They proposed a smart hierarchical algorithm where a top-level manager infers the preferences of lower-level workers and then offers tailored contracts in the form of bonuses to encourage cooperation. The manager's goal was to maximize overall performance while limiting the payments made, balancing efficiency with incentive costs. Their results show that a principal can learn optimal dynamic schemes in real time even when workers are strategic and hold private information, illustrating how computational approaches can be powerful in dynamic incentive design.

More recently, researchers have begun cracking the multi-layer dynamic contracting problem in full generality. Emma Hubert (2023) extended the earlier one-period model of Sung (2015), which had a principal, a top manager, and multiple workers in one period, into a true continuous-time setting. Her findings highlight a new insight that appears only in a continuous-time, multi-layer context: when an intermediate manager has control over an aggregate outcome, they effectively control its risk as well as its mean, and so the optimal contract for that manager must reward or penalize risk in addition to output. Essentially, if the middle manager can influence how stable the output the team produces is, the principal should tie their pay to risk measures in addition to the performance itself. Hubert's analysis showed that extending linear contracts, which work for single-agent cases, is suboptimal in a hierarchical system. In fact, the contract for the top manager needs an extra component that effectively penalizes them for high volatility, and rewards them for stability on top of the usual share in the total output. Moreover, it indicated that a manager's contract might also depend on other agents' performances in addition to their own subordinates to provide balanced incentives across the team. Another frontier is the application of multi-layer contracting to networked systems. Cao, Gao, and Huang (2025) analyze a federated learning hierarchy involving a task publisher at the top, local model owners as managers, and data workers as agents. Through this design, they designed the following framework: a Stackelberg game (where players move in a leader-follower sequence) between the publisher and managers, and a multi-agent decision process between managers and workers. By solving the lower game with deep reinforcement learning, they designed an adaptive hierarchical incentive mechanism that targeted the whole of the organization by just focusing on one segment. This study extends dynamic contracting to cases where multiple agents operate at each tier under uncertainty, underscoring the connection between continuous-time contracting and machine learning.

Another closely related study in finance is by Keppo, Touzi, and Zuo (2020), who modeled a hierarchy with an investor (principal), fund partner (intermediate), and fund manager (agent) — essentially a three-layer principal-agent problem in a continuous-time asset management setting. In the model, the partner chooses not only how much effort to exert but can also influence the risk of the investment portfolio, an approach drawn from the general framework of Cvitanic et al. (2017). The key takeaway from the paper is that in such financial hierarchies, the optimal contract often gives the intermediate partner a stake in the outcome and exposure to risk taken, aligning their incentives with the investor's returns and risk preferences — again underscoring that dual-role agents need multidimensional incentives in continuous time.

Despite all these advances, dynamic incentive theory and hierarchy theory have only recently begun intersecting, and many gaps remain. Miller & Yang showed that under special conditions, we can handle many layers simplistically, while Hubert showcased that continuous time brings new considerations for intermediaries. However, most of these models so far have imposed significant assumptions, or are applicable only to specific contexts. Some assume linear or restrictive contracts, which miss important features like risk-based terms. Many assume that all levels observe the same aggregate output process for simplification, which may not be realistic if different levels have their own performance measures. And with a few exceptions, all works treat either an infinitely large hierarchy under special conditions (Miller & Yang's approach), or a small hierarchy that requires advanced mathematics to solve optimally (Hubert's approach).

It is also worth noting that two classic works, though not cited earlier in this review, remain relevant. Sethi et al. (2008) review of hierarchical controls in stochastic manufacturing systems demonstrated the value of dividing organizations into hierarchical layers. Simon (1962) provides a broader perspective in his "Architecture of Complexity," where he explains why hierarchical structures arise naturally in complex systems and why they offer an advantage over flat systems (few middle layers). Together, these works explain the importance of hierarchies in managing complexity, and more importantly, how they can form naturally since it is a recurring factor that emerges in diverse organizations. Specifically, natural hierarchies are more favorable than unnatural ones since the former require less enforcement and money to maintain, making them more sustainable overall.

Additionally, Gryglewics and Mayer (2023) compared delegated monitoring vs delegated contracting, two paradigms in a hierarchy, within a dynamic setting. Essentially, this involved examining whether a principal should simply monitor a middle agent's performance, with that agent handling the lower worker, or write detailed contracts with both. They highlight the importance of the intermediate agent's dual role as both a supervisor and subordinate, as the way the intermediate's incentives are structured (e.g. whether the principal gives them autonomy to contact with the worker, or keeps control) can lead to differing outcomes. Taken together, the literature shows that while models of multi-layer contracting have become increasingly sophisticated, the central challenge remains how to design incentives that account for the intermediate agent's dual role and preserve alignment across the entire hierarchy.
